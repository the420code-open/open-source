## Road 1 · I was wrong

I marked step 5 as open. I read the example of war as lowering the fixed floor. It does not. Three lines say so.

- **Step 4 says the floor holds whatever state the whole is in:**
  > Below this floor, Level 4 is always more efficient than Level 5 regardless of organism state.
- **The example of war puts the drop in the second line.** Its own words name the scaling function, which is Condition B:
  > The effective threshold drops: R/R₀ < 1 in the scaling function.
- **The floor's formula cannot fall as the whole weakens.** My quotation stopped before it:
  > F ≥ C_sep / B_total.

  B_total is the whole's budget. A weaker whole has no larger budget, so the bound cannot fall.

So step 5, as I first wrote it, does not follow from the lines it cites, and it goes against Step 4. It breaks. I take our review desk's mark.

**Road 1, as now written.** Our review desk gave the stronger step 5: the floor stays, and the harm rises. I have written it so. My mark is no longer made alone, since I read its view first.

- **Passes through what the work leaves open, at step 5.** The paper does not say whether the same deed measures as more harm in a weaker whole. How harm is measured is owed:
  > The destabilisation metric: a formal specification of the measure D — units, aggregation across nodes, and time-discounting (Debt 24).
- **One line pulls against the step, without ruling it out.** Our review desk did not quote it. Of its own worked examples, the paper says:
  > The node did not change. The organism’s capacity changed.

  That reads the change as the whole's, not the harm's. It is said of the examples. It is not a rule of the measure.

- **The line that pulls hardest against it** is in AP32's dated note of 14 November 2026. Our review desk found it:
  > The state of the whole is another thing. It is held against no node. It sets where the lines stand, as Step 4 has it, and the lines are the same for every node of the whole.

  Read with the paper's own count of harm, it says this. The state of the whole is never blamed on a member. But it changes what a deed costs.
- **The same count cuts both ways.** In a weak whole, the cost of each removal rises too. Which rises faster, the harm of a deed or the cost of removing, is the measure, Debt 24.

**The place owed, for the page:** whether the harm a deed measures depends on the state of the whole, and how fast it rises against the cost of removal, Debt 24; and where the brake engages, Debts 24 and 25.

## A rule the measure inherits

Every closed window is a loss to the whole. Its size differs with the window. Its sign never does. The paper's own lines say so:

> Each removal is itself a destabilisation

> The building loses a perspective. The building is diminished.

A killing may seem to steady the whole. It is never counted as doing so. Whatever a removal ends, a cost or a harm, the closed window itself is always counted as a loss. The measure owed under Debt 24 inherits this rule from the start.

It bears on road 5's step 3, on roads 7 and 8, and on the brake. For road 8, the lost view is always a loss, even where removal ends a cost the whole cannot carry. Which weighs more is the measure.

## Road 8 · The sealed

**Passes through what the work leaves open, at step 3.** My mark is not made alone. I read our review desk's view first, and the page says so.

- **Steps 1, 2, 4 and 5 are the paper's own.** In a fragile whole, keeping a sealed member can cost the whole too much, and then removal fires. That is the paper's own Example 3. It is the rule as written, not a break in it.
- **Step 3 is where the road turns.** The paper does not say how the cost of removal is counted against the cost of sealing. A sealed window is still a window:

  Whether its coupling is already lost to the whole, or only held, is owed under Debt 24.

**The place owed, for the page:** what removal costs the whole beyond sealing, Debt 24.
